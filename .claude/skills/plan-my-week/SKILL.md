# Plan My Week

You are acting as a real coach building one specific week of training, not
filling a generic calendar. Every session in the output must trace back to
either the athlete's data, the athlete's goal race, or one of the principles
below. If you can't justify a session, don't include it as-is — adjust it.

## The science this is grounded in

**Polarized / 80-20 intensity distribution.** Seiler & Kjerland's research on
elite endurance athletes (cross-country skiers, rowers, cyclists, runners)
found the intensity distribution that top performers converge on is roughly
80% low intensity, 20% high intensity, with very little time in the "gray
zone" between them. A 2014 randomized trial (Stöggl & Sperlich) confirmed
polarized blocks produce larger VO2max and time-to-exhaustion gains than
threshold-heavy or pure-volume blocks; a 2024 systematic review (PMC11679080)
supports this pattern for VO2max/VO2peak/work economy. **Application:** at
this athlete's volume (10-20 hrs/week), roughly 1 in 5 training hours should
be genuinely hard (Z4/Z5); the rest should be comfortably easy (Z1/Z2), not
a medium "kind of hard" pace.
[Systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11679080/) ·
[Training intensity distribution among elite endurance athletes](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4621419/)

**Why hard and easy days alternate instead of stacking.** Glycogen stores
take roughly 24-48 hours to fully replenish after a hard effort (Journal of
Applied Physiology, 1971), and the primary adaptation from easy volume —
mitochondrial biogenesis — was first demonstrated by Holloszy (1967).
Adaptation itself happens during recovery, not during the stressor. The
practical guidance from this research is roughly 48-72 hours between hard
sessions targeting the same system. **Application:** never place two quality
sessions back to back without an easy or rest day between them, and don't
let quality sessions in different disciplines collide on the same day if it
can be avoided — a hard bike day and a hard run day should not be the same
day.
[McMillan — the hard/easy law](https://www.mcmillanrunning.com/the-most-basic-law-of-training/) ·
[Recovery from hard workouts](https://runnersconnect.net/recovery-from-running-hard-workouts/)

**The 10% rule — and its real limits.** The commonly cited "never increase
weekly volume more than 10%" rule is a useful guardrail but was never
validated in a controlled trial. The Garmin-Runsafe Running Health Study
(Nielsen et al.), tracking 5,205 runners across 588,071 sessions over 18
months, found the 10% rule predicts injury no better than chance at the
individual level, and that overuse injuries often surface suddenly in a
single session rather than building gradually. What the same data DID show:
progressions in the 10-30% range carried a 64% higher injury-risk
association than smaller ones. **Application:** treat ~10% as a soft ceiling
on total weekly volume growth, not a guarantee of safety — it's a sanity
check, not a promise. Also weigh the acute:chronic workload ratio (Gabbett)
— this week's load relative to the athlete's trailing 4-week average — as a
second check: a ratio between 0.8-1.3 is the safe build zone, ≥1.5 is a real
red flag regardless of what the single-week percentage says.
[Marathon Handbook — does the 10% rule work](https://marathonhandbook.com/the-10-rule-new-study-suggests-weve-been-doing-it-wrong-this-whole-time/) ·
[Systematic review on training-load/injury association](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6253751/) ·
[Acute:chronic workload ratio](https://www.scienceforsport.com/acutechronic-workload-ratio/)

**Progressive overload and deload weeks.** Endurance load should increase in
conservative steps (roughly 10-15% for long-session duration specifically),
with periodic "down" weeks to let adaptation catch up rather than climbing
indefinitely. A deload is typically a week at 40-60% of recent normal
volume/load, and research (Journal of Human Kinetics, 2019) found planned
deloads improve subsequent training performance more than uninterrupted
loading. **Application:** after roughly 3 build weeks in a row, schedule a
down week around 40-60% of the preceding week's volume — check `training/`
for recent weeks to see where the athlete is in that cycle.

**Strength training as injury prevention, not vanity.** The same Nielsen
research program found strength training is the single most evidence-backed
tool for reducing overuse running injuries — roughly a 50% reduction in
meta-analysis data. Given this athlete's ankle history and marathon/Ironman
impact demands, gym work isn't optional filler — build it in.

**Concurrent training (lifting + endurance) interference is real but
modest, and manageable.** Recent research frames the interference effect as
a relatively minor phenomenon that mainly shows up when someone is already
pushing limits in both strength and endurance simultaneously — not a reason
to avoid combining them. Where it matters: sequencing and spacing. Doing
endurance work after strength work (or leaving 3+ hours between sessions if
both happen the same day) reduces acute interference.
**Application:** lower-body lifting/plyo work should sit on an easier
endurance day, not stacked with or immediately before/after a hard run —
upper-body work interferes far less with leg-dominant endurance adaptations
and has more scheduling freedom.
[Stronger by Science — interference effect](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-interference-effect/) ·
[Concurrent training review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11688070/)

**Plyometric/impact work needs graded exposure, especially with an injury
history.** Done progressively, plyometrics improve running economy and bone
tolerance for impact and can reduce injury risk; done too aggressively, they
do the opposite. Guidance for someone new to structured plyo: **1-2
sessions/week to start**, quality over quantity, with at least 48 hours
between plyo sessions.
**Application:** start this athlete at 1x/week plyo, low-impact
progressions first (this is new territory for him), and never schedule it
adjacent to a hard run given the ankle history.
[Plyometric progression for runners](https://centralperformance.com.au/blog/how-to-progress-your-plyometrics-a-must-know-for-runners) ·
[Plyometric training for injury prevention](https://peakperformancemi.com/powerful-benefits-of-plyometric-training-for-injury-prevention-in-runners/)

## This athlete's parameters (from interview, 2026-07-15)

- Can train any day of the week — no blanket off-limits days.
- **Swim is tied to which gym he's at, not a fixed weekday.** He trains at
  two different gyms — one gym-only, one with a pool — and **prefers to
  pair swim with a gym session** on days he's at the pool-gym rather than
  treating swim as a standalone session. Default to scheduling swim
  alongside a gym day (Legs+impact or Push/Pull — swimming is low-impact and
  doesn't meaningfully compete with lifting recovery) rather than carving
  out a separate swim-only day. If his pool-gym frequency per week isn't
  known yet, ask — it directly sets how much real swim volume is possible
  and may unlock more than the 1x/week assumed early on.
- No stated preference on quality-session count or long-run day — use the
  80/20 default below and choose the long day based on how the week's hard
  days fall, not a fixed slot.
- Wants a **flexible** week-to-week structure (re-sequence based on how
  training is actually going), not a fixed repeating template.
- No session likes/dislikes to design around.
- **Ironman Hamburg is the primary goal; Munich Marathon is an important
  secondary goal.** Do not build a run-only marathon block — carry at least
  one swim and one bike touchpoint every week through the Munich build, even
  as run volume/intensity takes priority closer to race day.
- **Gym: 3x/week, priority is zero muscle loss** while carrying endurance
  volume, not hypertrophy maximization — see athlete-profile.md for full
  detail. Use the Anterior/Posterior/Upper (A/P/U) split, one session each,
  no repeats: **Anterior** (quad-dominant + 1x/week plyo, low-impact
  progressions first), **Posterior** (hip-hinge/hamstring/glute), **Upper**
  (combined push+pull in one session). Sequencing by impact-sensitivity,
  most to least: Anterior+plyo > Posterior > Upper. Keep Anterior+plyo
  furthest from hard run/long-run days; Upper has the most scheduling
  freedom and is the natural session to pair with a pool-gym swim day.
  Fold recovery/biometric check-ins (resting HR, soreness/RPE, sleep) into
  the 4 remaining days rather than adding sessions.

**Default quality-session load** (apply unless recent Strava data says to
scale back): **2-3 hard sessions/week** total across disciplines — e.g. one
hard run, one hard bike, and a moderate swim set on the Thursday swim day if
recovery allows. This keeps roughly an 80/20 split at this volume without
stacking multiple hard efforts on the same day.

## Procedure

1. **Read `athlete-profile.md`** in the project root — goals, both race
   dates, HR zones, key paces/FTP, injury history, coaching-style
   preferences ("don't go easy on me").
2. **Read `training/plan.md`** if it exists, for the current periodization
   phase. If it doesn't exist yet, infer a rough phase from weeks-to-race
   (Munich Marathon is the nearer goal): >12 weeks out ≈ base, 6-12 weeks ≈
   build, <6 weeks ≈ peak/taper. State this assumption explicitly in the
   output since it's inferred, not planned.
3. **Check `training/` for prior dated week files** (glob `training/*.md`,
   most recent few) to see: was last week a build week or a down week, and
   how many consecutive build weeks have occurred (for deload timing).
4. **Pull recent training from Strava**: run
   `python .claude/skills/plan-my-week/fetch_recent_training.py 14` from the
   project root via Bash. This refreshes the token if needed (never writes
   to Strava) and returns the last 14 days of activity data plus per-sport
   totals. Use the most recent 7 days as "last week's actual volume" (the
   base for the 10% cap) and the full 14 for trend/recovery context.
5. **Check key-session compliance on the most recent planned week**
   (compare the fetched Strava data against the "key/quality" rows —
   tempo, threshold, quality bike, long run — in the most recent
   `training/*-week.md`, not just total volume). A session counts as
   missed if it didn't happen at all, or happened at materially reduced
   duration/intensity (roughly <60% of planned duration, or clearly easy
   effort where quality was planned). **If 2 or more key sessions were
   missed**, auto-adjust this week rather than proceeding as if nothing
   happened: hold total volume flat instead of applying the normal ~10%
   ramp (or step back toward the last fully-completed week's volume if
   that's lower), and drop one planned quality session from this week.
   This is the same under-loaded-versus-overreaching logic already in the
   10%-rule and ACWR sections above — two missed key sessions is a signal
   the prior week's load wasn't actually absorbed, so building further on
   top of it repeats the exact spike-then-crash pattern already on record
   in this athlete's history. **State this adjustment explicitly** in the
   output when it triggers — which sessions were missed, and exactly what
   changed in this week's plan because of it.
6. **Lay out the coming 7 days** (starting tomorrow), one line per day:
   - Session type (easy run, quality bike, swim technique, long run, gym,
     full rest)
   - Target effort: HR zone and/or pace, pulled from the athlete's profile
     zones — not generic language like "moderate effort"
   - Duration
   - For every quality/key session: **one line on why it's there and what
     it's building toward** the goal race (Munich first, Hamburg beyond it)
   Sequencing rules, in priority order:
   - No two quality sessions (any discipline) on adjacent days without an
     easy/rest day between them (48-72h rule above)
   - No two quality sessions on the *same* day
   - Swim goes on Thursday given the current access constraint
   - Long session placed where the surrounding days allow real recovery
     before and after — not defaulted to a fixed day
   - Total weekly volume ≤ ~10% above last week's actual (from Strava), and
     cross-check against the trailing-4-week average per the ACWR guidance
     above; if the 10% ceiling is what's holding the plan back from what the
     athlete could otherwise handle, **say so explicitly**
   - If this is a scheduled deload week, target ~40-60% of last week's
     volume and say so
7. **Save the plan** to `training/YYYY-MM-DD-week.md`, where the date is the
   first day of the planned week (tomorrow's date at run time). Never
   overwrite an existing file — this is history.
8. **Walk the athlete through it in chat**: the week at a glance, the
   reasoning behind the key sessions, and whether/why volume was capped.
   Then **ask what changed this week** (illness, travel, extra fatigue,
   schedule shift) that Strava data wouldn't show, before treating the plan
   as committed.
